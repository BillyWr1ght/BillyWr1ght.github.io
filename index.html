About Me

William
Wright

"Engineering isn't just what I study — it's how I think about every problem I encounter."

I'm a student driven by a genuine curiosity about how things work and a persistent urge to build things myself. My interest in engineering started with a simple question: how can precise geometry create complex motion? That question led me to learn CAD, and CAD led me to a workshop, and the workshop turned into a habit of taking ideas from concept to physical reality.

I spend most of my engineering time in Onshape and SolidWorks — not just sketching parts, but thinking through assemblies, tolerances, and how pieces move relative to each other. I believe the process of designing a mechanism teaches you more about physics and math than any textbook can.

Beyond CAD, I'm deeply interested in robotics. There's something uniquely satisfying about a system that responds to its environment — where mechanical design, electronics, and programming have to cooperate to produce a useful result. Through robotics, I've learned that engineering is fundamentally a collaborative discipline.

I document every project carefully — not because I have to, but because reflecting on what worked and what failed is where most of the real learning happens. Every broken prototype and every revised design has taught me something I couldn't have learned any other way.

My Approach

Learning by Building

Every project starts with a question I don't know the answer to. I sketch ideas on paper first — usually rough and messy — then move to CAD once the geometry makes sense in my head. The move from digital model to physical prototype is always humbling. Parts don't fit exactly. Tolerances matter more than you'd think. The mechanism binds or wobbles in a way the simulation didn't predict.

That gap between the model and reality is where engineering actually happens. Closing it through testing, redesigning, and iterating has taught me more than any single success ever has.

My Philosophy

Document Everything

I treat every project as a record of how my thinking evolved. Good documentation isn't just about showing finished work — it's about being honest about what didn't work and why. A failed prototype that gets analyzed and improved is more impressive than a first attempt that happened to succeed.

This portfolio is part of that documentation habit. I want anyone who looks at my work to understand not just what I built, but how I thought through it and what I'd do differently next time.

Project Overview

The Geneva mechanism is one of the most elegant solutions in mechanical engineering for converting continuous rotational motion into precise, intermittent step motion. The drive wheel — with a single pin — engages a slotted wheel, advancing it exactly one slot per revolution before locking it in place. The result is a mechanism that indexes with precision and repeatability without needing complex electronics or sensors.

My objective was to understand this mechanism from first principles: study the geometry, design all parts parametrically in Onshape, 3D print a working prototype, and iterate until the mechanism ran smoothly with minimal play and backlash.

Final Outcome: A fully functional 4-slot Geneva mechanism with smooth, repeatable indexing, a locking arc that prevents unintended rotation, and a parametric CAD model that can be scaled for different applications.

Assembly Overview
02

Why I Built It

I first encountered the Geneva mechanism in a video about classic mechanical watches, where it's used to control how the film advances frame by frame. The geometry fascinated me — there's no sensor, no motor controller, no software. Just the shape of the parts determines the entire motion profile.

Engineering Principles: This project demonstrates intermittent motion, geometric constraint, and mechanical timing — all fundamental concepts in mechanism design that appear in everything from industrial indexing tables to vending machines.

The Challenge: Getting a printed mechanism to operate without binding requires understanding tolerance, clearance, and the relationship between surface finish and friction. This is where the gap between CAD and physical reality becomes most instructive.

Core Principle
Intermittent motion without electronics
Real-World Applications
Film projectors, indexing tables, vending machines, watch escapements

03

Project Timeline

Idea & Research Click to expand
Studied the geometric principles of the Geneva mechanism. Researched slot count variations (4, 5, 6 slot), drive pin geometry, and locking arc design. Watched mechanism animations to understand the full motion cycle before touching CAD.
Sketches & Concepts Click to expand
Drew multiple hand sketches exploring slot geometry, center distance, pin radius, and base plate layout. Sketching helped me understand the geometric relationships before translating them to parametric CAD constraints.
CAD Design Click to expand
Built fully parametric models in Onshape. Made center distance and pin radius master variables so slot geometry updates automatically. Created the drive wheel, Geneva wheel, base plate, axles, and motor mount as separate parts in a single assembly.
First Prototype Click to expand
Printed Revision 1 in PLA. The mechanism moved but bound up at the entry and exit of each slot due to insufficient clearance. The pin-slot engagement was too tight because I hadn't accounted for FDM print tolerances (~0.3mm over-extrusion on inner surfaces).
Testing & Iteration Click to expand
Revised clearances from 0.1mm to 0.35mm. Widened slot entry chamfer. Added 0.5mm draft to slot walls to reduce contact area. Revision 3 ran smoothly at low speed but wobbled at higher RPM due to axle slop — added a second bearing bore to constrain axle.
Final Product Click to expand
Revision 4 achieved consistent, smooth indexing with less than 1° of backlash in the locked position. The mechanism runs at up to ~60 RPM on the drive wheel without binding. Parametric model documented and ready for scaling.

Manufacturing & Prototyping

All parts were printed on an FDM printer in PLA. Print orientation was critical — the Geneva wheel was printed flat to maximize slot surface quality and minimize layer lines in the engagement surfaces. Drive wheel printed vertically to keep the pin perpendicular to the print bed.

Revision 1 revealed the tolerance problem immediately. The slots were too tight, and the locking arc had slight layer gaps that caused the wheel to rock. Revision 2 opened tolerances and added 0.5mm chamfers at slot entries. Revision 3 addressed axle wobble. Revision 4 was the final production version.

07

Testing

Testing was done by hand-spinning the drive wheel at varying speeds and observing behavior at three points: slot entry, mid-engagement, and exit. I noted binding, noise, and wobble at each phase. Later tests were done with a small DC motor at controlled RPM.

Key findings: slot entry chamfers dramatically reduced the "click" at engagement. The locking arc needed a 0.15mm relief to prevent surface contact drag during locked phases. Final mechanism achieves consistent indexing with no perceptible backlash in the locked position.

09

Reflection

💡 What I Learned

FDM tolerances are non-trivial. You can't design to CAD nominal dimensions and expect perfect fits — you have to design to the process. I also learned to think geometrically about constraint: where a mechanism has play depends entirely on the geometric relationship between mating surfaces, not just individual part accuracy.

⚠️ Challenges I Faced

The biggest challenge was the binding at slot entry/exit. I initially thought the slots were too narrow, but after measuring the printed parts I realized the problem was layer adhesion causing slight surface bulging at the slot edges. Chamfering the entry geometry solved it more effectively than simply increasing clearance.

🔧 What I'd Improve

I'd like to design a version with metal axles and printed bearings to eliminate the wobble at higher RPM without relying on tight clearance bores. I'd also add an encoder disk to measure actual indexing accuracy and document the error profile as a function of drive speed.

📈 Skills Developed

Parametric CAD in Onshape (master variables, feature lists), tolerance analysis, FDM design principles, iterative prototyping, geometric constraint thinking, and technical documentation. This project also gave me a much better intuition for how mechanism design balances precision against manufacturability.

Working as a Team

Robotics has taught me that the most complex engineering challenges aren't purely technical — they require communication, division of work, and integrating subsystems designed by different people. A drivetrain and an arm that work perfectly in isolation have to work together under time pressure.
